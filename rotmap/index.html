Accents, apostrophes and near-misses are all forgiven — type haesema and Hā-aēšema comes back. A hit stays lit until you click it off; hold Shift to keep several lit at once.

How routing works Click hexes to add waypoints to the active route. Right-click a hex to add one there without clicking the map at all — which is how you keep building a route while another panel has the map's clicks, the Isochrone one especially — or to start a fresh route there, or to remove the last waypoint from this one; right-click a hex the route stops in to remove that waypoint, wherever it sits in the route. Remove last does the same for touchscreens, where there is no right button. Ctrl+Z undoes. Click a route's swatch to recolour it, double-click its name to rename; its ⋯ menu — or a right-click on the row, or on the title bar of the breakdown card, which describes the same route — will duplicate it, empty it of waypoints, or copy its march and its column to the clipboard as text you can keep and paste back. A route that sets out from a hex a token is standing on takes that token's colour, so a legion's march is drawn in the legion's own colour. In the readout, right-click a step to force the march from there, end the push, or split the route in two at that hex — each plants a waypoint, since the pace is solved leg by leg.

Spread

How the isochrone works While this panel is open, clicking the map moves the selected origin — no waypoints are placed. Elsewhere, Shift+click does the same. Right-click a hex to make a new origin there, move the selected one, remove the one standing on it, or clear the lot — the same three things this list offers, put where you are already pointing. The route entries are on that menu too, so you can go on adding waypoints to a march while the shading you are plotting it against stays up. With more than one origin each area is outlined in its own colour, and contested ground goes to whichever origin reaches it in fewer days; hovering a hex names the runner-up and says by how much it lost. Click an origin's swatch to recolour it, double-click its name to rename; its ⋯ menu (or a right-click on the row) will move it, duplicate it, or copy and paste its column. Each origin marches under its own column, set below — so the line between two of them moves if you change either force.

Shading is by subhex, not by hex: where a coast or a major river has cut a hex in two, each half is asked separately and only the half that can actually be reached is shaded. A bay a fleet can enter no longer lights up the shore an army can't march to. Water is shaded only for a column that could actually be afloat: one with a fleet, or with leave to go and secure one. A column with neither is landlocked, and open sea is left out of its shading altogether. Whether the days stretch to the seven a fleet costs is the search's business, not a separate guess.

No column here — a message and a rumour travel as the crow flies, so nothing about the force that carries them changes the answer.

The origin is where it is happening — a siege, a battle, a landing; what is shaded is where you could station a force and still have it arrive in time.

How a relief army works The place to be relieved is the subhex the origin stands on, taken exactly as placed — on a hex a coast or a major river has cut up, that is the half you clicked, water included, and the tooltip's header names which. Relief is then costed to that piece of ground: a force can be a hex away and still pay a day to reach a bay its ships cannot enter from where they are.

Two journeys in opposite directions, not one. News of it reaches the force as the crow flies, at the speed set above; then the column marches back over the roads, under the column and conditions set below. A hex is shaded by the total — a 1-day rumour and a 3-day march is a 4 — and hovering it breaks that back out into its two legs, with the unrounded figures in brackets, since which leg is eating the budget is what you can do something about. A hex held back by the march wants a road; one held back by the news wants a courier posted rather than a garrison moved.

Each leg is billed in whole days, and then they are added. Word arriving in the afternoon still costs that day, and the column forms up and sets out on the next one; so half a day of riding and half a day of marching are two days here, not one, and nothing but the origin's own hex can come in under two.

The march is costed inwards, in the direction it is actually made. A hex is paid for by the step that enters it, so relieving a fortress in the mountains pays the mountain on the way in — a march solved outward from the origin would never pay it at all — and the force sets out under the column of the hex it is standing in.

How long you actually have

For a siege, the clock does not stop when the walls do. Taking a stronghold in hand after it falls costs 5 in-game days for a fortress — 1 IRL day — while a town takes 1d6 × 5 (1–6 IRL days, 3½ on average) and a city 2d6 × 5 (2–12 IRL days, 7 on average). Add however long you expect the place to hold out before that, and you have the real budget: a force that arrives while the enemy is still counting the grain has not arrived late.

For a battle it is whatever you judge the line can hold for, which the map cannot tell you. The 4 days this opens on is a working default, not a rule — set it to whatever the thing in front of you is worth, and remember that a town's 1d6 is a gamble you are making before the roll: 4 covers a town two times in three.

How tokens work Right-click a hex and choose Mark as to drop a counter on it — I to XIV, or text of your own. After that: click it to cycle its colour, drag it to another hex, right-click it to rename, recolour, split off a detachment (V → V'a → V'b) or remove. The rows above answer the right button the same way. Ctrl+Z takes back a placement, a move, a colour or a deletion — one stack shared with the routes and the drawing, so it always undoes whatever you did last. On a touchscreen, press Place token and then tap a hex; hold a token for its menu.

Using the tools

Placing points Click to add snapped points — corners, edge midpoints, centres, sub-points, and the vertices of features you have already drawn, so new lines connect precisely — at most one hex ahead per click, so nothing gets skipped. Enter or double-click finishes, Esc cancels, Ctrl+Z undoes. Hold Alt for free placement, or E to snap anywhere along the nearest hex edge.
Coast Locked to a single hex: the first click picks the hex, and points snap only to that hex's corners, edges, centre and sub-points (hold E to snap anywhere along an edge). Points also snap back onto the line you are drawing — once you have three, the starting node turns into a teal ring, and clicking it closes the loop and finishes the line. That is how you draw an island smaller than a hex. Then click the side that is sea. Coast lines act as barriers, so two coasts can enclose an inner sea; works on land and sea hexes alike. Shift+click a coast later to re-pick its sea side.
Map painting Paint who holds a subhex, on the Borders or the Warlords layer. Reaching for the tool switches that layer on if it was off — you cannot paint a map you cannot see. The first time it does, the Layers panel opens with the row it ticked flashing, so it is clear what changed and where to change it back; after that it switches the layer on quietly. The palette is whatever colours that scan actually uses, commonest first, named where the names are known — so a realm added to a scan turns up here on its own. Click to paint, drag to sweep, and click the same colour again to lift it and let the scan speak. Nobody clears ground the scan claims, which nothing else can do. A hand-painted subhex beats the scan, the spit inheritance and the warlords overlay alike, and travels in your features JSON.
Custom colour… mixes one the scan never used — for a realm that did not exist when the scan was drawn — opening on whatever is currently loaded, since mixing is usually an adjustment. It joins the palette straight away, so it can be picked up again without reopening the dialog. Pick from map arms the dropper: the next click loads the colour under it instead of painting, reading what the map is showing there — an override, an inherited spit and a warlord over the borders all answer alike. Ground nobody holds picks up Nobody. Esc disarms.
Double-click a colour's name to rename it. The Warlords scan ships with a legend of ten; the Borders washes have none and go by their hex code until you say otherwise. A name is kept per layer and per colour, beats the legend, and shows in the hover readout as well as the palette — clear the text to fall back to the legend, then to the hex. Names travel in your features JSON with the paint. On the readout Borders speaks only where you have named a wash — unnamed, it would be a hex code, which tells nobody anything — plus the realms that appear there in their own colour rather than the empire's, which today means the Blue Scarves. Name a polity once and every hex of it answers from then on.
A named realm can also be written across its own ground. The A button on the Borders and Warlords rows of the Layers panel lays each name along the land it holds — fitted to the shape, so it runs the length of the country and bends with it rather than sitting straight across the middle. Only named colours appear, which makes this a third reading of the same names the palette and the readout give. A realm split by sea gets its name on each piece large enough to carry it, and where two names would land on top of one another the larger upper layer keeps its and the other yields — first by being set smaller, and only then by going without, since a name nudged aside to fit would be labelling somebody else's country. With Warlords up, a legion's name beats the name of the imperial ground it sits on: the paint you can see there is the legion's, so the name should be too. A name the two layers share, like the Blue Scarves, is written once. Off by default: the layer is already colouring this ground, and the lettering is a second answer to a question you may not be asking.
Locally there is a Realm labels panel on the rail, which is where all of this is actually decided. Eight ways of finding the line a name is written along — a straight axis, a parabola, a fitted arc, arcs all sharing one distant centre, and four kinds of walk along the country's own spine — and a slider for every threshold in the fitting, answering live as you drag. It also says what the last fit did: how many names were drawn, how many were dropped for colliding, and which algorithm each one came from. The face and the ink are on the same panel; three of the faces are fetched from the web and are auditions only, requested the first time you pick one and never otherwise. None of it exists on the published map, which takes the defaults — the settings this panel was used to arrive at.
Stronghold Click to place or restore the marker within its hex; Shift+click toggles coastal/port — a blue ring means a port, where embarking and disembarking are possible. A hex's right-click menu sets the class: ordinary, fortress (the same marker with a purple square set inside the white), or major (the same marker drawn larger). Size is a degree and the square is a kind, so the two say different things and only one class holds at a time. Sea- and river-side strongholds are ports by default, and Shift+click overrides that either way for the exceptions.
Label Click any hex to name or rename it, which works on datasheet strongholds too; clear the text to fall back to the datasheet name. A commandery is named for the grandest settlement inside it, so renaming the right stronghold renames the commandery around it.
The names come off the map without the markers: the A button on the Strongholds row of the Layers panel switches the lettering alone, leaving every disc where it was. On by default — a stronghold you cannot name is of limited use — but on a crowded stretch of coast it is the names that collide rather than the markers, and reading the shape of the ground is easier with them off. They are drawn under the realm lettering: a town's name is the name of a place, a realm's is a claim on a whole stretch of ground, and the larger claim reads over the smaller. The markers themselves stay on top of both, being small and being what you click.
Erase Click a node to remove just it, click between nodes to cut that segment (which splits the line), Shift+click to remove a whole line, or drag across the map to wipe features and strongholds as you go. Clicking a stronghold marker removes it — including datasheet strongholds, which are hidden persistently; Ctrl+Z brings them back.
